Force Fields & Molecular Dynamics — An Interactive Field Guide

Why force fields exist at all

You cannot watch a protein fold or a liquid flow with a microscope — the atomic scale is too small to see and too fast to catch. Molecular Dynamics (MD) works around this: integrate Newton's second law for every particle and you get a complete movie of where every atom is at every instant.

mi​dt2d2ri​​=fi​=−∇ri​​U(r1​,…,rN​)

This is N coupled, second-order, nonlinear differential equations — analytically unsolvable for anything beyond a few particles. The only real question is where U comes from.

The trade-off that defines everything

Ab initio MD buys chemical accuracy at the price of size and time. Classical force fields buy scale and speed at the price of chemical reactivity. Neither is "more correct" — they answer different questions, and the choice of tool follows the question.

Anatomy of the master equation

A force field is two things bundled together: a functional form for U(r1​,…,rN​) and a set of fitted parameters. The canonical class-I expression splits U into six physically distinct contributions:

U​=bond stretchbonds∑​21​kb​(r−r0​)2​​+angle bendangles∑​21​ka​(θ−θ0​)2​​+dihedraltorsions∑​2Vn​​[1+cos(nφ−γ)]​​+planarityimproper∑​Vimp​(ω)​​+van der WaalsLJ∑​4εij​[(rij​σij​​)12−(rij​σij​​)6]​​+electrostaticselec∑​rij​qi​qj​​​​​

The first four terms sculpt each molecule's shape; the last two decide how molecules — or distant atoms in the same one — interact. The parameters come from quantum-mechanical calculations (geometries, torsion scans, electrostatic potentials) or from fitting to diffraction, IR/Raman spectroscopy, and thermodynamic data.

The bonded terms

Bond stretch models every covalent bond as a harmonic spring around its equilibrium length. It is the most consequential approximation in classical MD:

Ubond​=21​kb​(r−r0​)2

Why classical MD cannot do chemistry

A parabola never flattens and never breaks. The harmonic bond becomes unreliable past ≈10% displacement and structurally cannot describe bond dissociation or formation. The Morse potential (dashed curve above) can — but it costs more and is rarely used in production force fields. This single choice is the main reason reactive chemistry requires AIMD, QM/MM, or ReaxFF-style potentials.

Angle bending gets the same harmonic treatment, or a trigonometric form that behaves better at extreme angles:

Ubend​=21​ka​(θ−θ0​)2or21​ka​(cosθ−cosθ0​)2

The Urey–Bradley correction adds a spring between the two outer atoms of an angle, purely to sharpen the fit to vibrational spectra.

Dihedrals are hundreds of times softer than stretching and generate conformational variety — the difference between a folded and unfolded chain:

Utors​=2Vn​​[1+cos(nφ−γ)]

Keeping it flat

Aromatic rings and carbonyl carbons need to stay planar, and an ordinary torsion cannot guarantee that. An improper torsion term penalizes the deviation ω from the flat configuration directly.

The non-bonded terms

Van der Waals combines a violent short-range repulsion with a weak long-range attraction — the 12-6 Lennard-Jones potential:

ULJ​(r)=4ε[(rσ​)12−(rσ​)6]

Why r−12? The r−6 attraction has a rigorous basis in London dispersion; the r−12 repulsion is chosen for pure computational convenience — it is simply (r−6)2, so it costs nothing extra. Some force fields (Buckingham, COMPASS 9-6, MMFF 14-7) soften the wall, mainly for organic molecular crystals.

Combining rules turn atomic parameters into pair parameters:

εij​=εi​εj​​,σij​=21​(σi​+σj​)orσi​σj​​

Electrostatics uses borrowed, non-measurable partial charges fit to reproduce a QM electrostatic potential:

Uelec​=rij​qi​qj​​

Charges are a modeling choice

Partial charges are not experimental observables — many charge-fitting schemes exist (RESP, CHELPG…) and they do not always agree. And because condensed-phase polarization is not explicitly present, non-polarizable water models quietly inflate their dipole (≈2.3–2.6 D vs. the gas-phase 1.85 D) to compensate on average.

The 1–4 exclusion rule prevents double counting: 1–2 and 1–3 pairs are excluded (already described by bond/angle terms), 1–4 pairs are scaled with a force-field-specific factor, and everything further apart interacts fully.

Never mix force fields

It is the specific combination of the dihedral term with the scaled 1–4 van der Waals and electrostatic terms that sets a molecule's real rotational barriers. Borrowing parameters from another force field for any one of those pieces silently breaks that balance.

The critical transformation: U→F

The bridge from energy to dynamics is the negative gradient. For the Lennard-Jones pair this is simple enough to write analytically (González Eq. 3.2):

fij​=48σ2ε​[(rij​σ​)14−21​(rij​σ​)8]rij​,fi​=j=i∑​fij​

Why this matters computationally

The force calculation is the most expensive step in MD: the naive approach costs 21​N(N−1) distance evaluations per step. Every acceleration strategy in MD exists to make this cheaper — never the other way around.

Boxes, cutoffs, and the long-range problem

Periodic boundary conditions kill the walls: an atom leaving one face re-enters through the opposite one. The surface-artifact fraction scales as N−1/3 — ≈49% of atoms sit at the surface for 103 atoms, but only ≈6% for 106, so PBC only become affordable at scale.

Cutoffs drop interactions beyond Rc​≤L/2:

Utrunc​(r)={ULJ(r),0,​r≤Rc​r>Rc​​

But the chopped-off tail is not free — and there is an analytic correction for it:

Utail​=38​πρσ3ε[31​(Rc​σ​)9−(Rc​σ​)3]

The tail is not optional

At typical liquid density and Rc​=2.5σ the tail correction is Utail​≈−0.535ε — roughly 10% of the total potential energy. And this correction applies only to short-range terms: Coulomb decays as 1/r, far too slowly to truncate at all.

Long-range electrostatics therefore need Ewald-style methods. Ewald splits the conditionally convergent lattice sum into a real-space part, a reciprocal-space part, and a self-energy term:

Ucoul​=Ureal​+Urecip​+Uself​

with Ureal​=21​∑ij​qi​qj​erfc(α​rij​)/rij​ and the reciprocal part built from the charge density ρ(k)=∑i​qi​eik⋅ri​. Particle-mesh descendants (PME, SPME, PPPM) bring the cost to O(NlogN). The cheap alternative — a reaction field continuum beyond the cutoff — is crude at typical 9–15 Å cutoffs.

Stepping through time

A plain Taylor expansion is numerically unstable. Verlet's trick: add the forward and backward expansions so the odd-order terms cancel:

ri​(t0​+Δt)=−ri​(t0​−Δt)+2ri​(t0​)+ai​(t0​)Δt2+O(Δt4)

The equivalent, more practical formulations are leap-frog and velocity-Verlet — the field's default because positions and velocities live at the same instant:

ri​(t0​+Δt)=ri​(t0​)+vi​(t0​)Δt+21​ai​(t0​)Δt2 vi​(t0​+Δt)=vi​(t0​)+21​[ai​(t0​)+ai​(t0​+Δt)]Δt

What makes an integrator trustworthy

Verlet-family algorithms are cheap (one force evaluation per step), time-reversible, and symplectic — they conserve phase-space volume, so energy stays bounded over long runs. That is why predictor-corrector schemes, more accurate per step but neither reversible nor symplectic, have fallen out of favor.

Choosing Δt — governed by the fastest motion you refuse to alias:

SystemTypical Δt
Lennard-Jones fluid (argon, near triple point)≈ 5 fs
Rigid molecules≈ 5 fs
Flexible molecules with C–H stretching0.5 – 1 fs

The sanity check on a NVE run: total energy should not drift — convention is ∣ΔE∣≤0.003 averaged over steps.

Ensembles and thermostats

Plain Newtonian integration conserves N, V, E — the microcanonical (NVE) ensemble. Experiments fix other things, so you usually need control:

EnsembleHeld fixedTypical use
NVEN,V,EProduction runs after equilibration
NVTN,V,TMatching a target temperature
NPTN,P,TEquilibration; finding the correct density

Temperature control comes in three flavors with very different guarantees:

Recommended workflow

Equilibrate with velocity scaling or tight Berendsen coupling to reach the target state quickly, then switch to NVE (or Nosé–Hoover) for the production run the analysis will actually use.

Why neutrons specifically

The spatial and temporal windows a neutron spectrometer probes line up almost exactly with what an MD box computes — the intermediate scattering functions Is​(Q,t) and S(Q,ω) are the go-to yardsticks for validating force fields, not just a nice-to-have comparison.

Free energy: binding, catalysis, and PMFs

A raw trajectory shows where atoms go, but most biophysical questions are thermodynamic: how tightly does a ligand bind, how high is a barrier, which conformer dominates? These all reduce to a free-energy difference ΔG — and entropy is never a simple time average, so MD reaches ΔG through sampling.

Alchemical routes transform state 0 into state 1 along an unphysical pathway. Free Energy Perturbation computes the Zwanzig average:

ΔA=−kB​Tln⟨exp(−kB​TU1​−U0​​)⟩0​

Thermodynamic integration replaces the exponential with an integral over the ensemble-averaged force along the coupling parameter λ:

ΔA=∫01​⟨∂λ∂U(λ)​⟩λ​dλ

Where this breaks

Every free-energy method inherits the force field's own errors — wrong van der Waals or electrostatic parameters produce a confidently wrong ΔG. Convergence also demands adequate sampling of every relevant conformational state, which slow biological motions make hard.

Machine-learned potentials

Classical force fields fix a functional form up front; ML potentials learn the energy/force mapping directly from QM reference data. Because raw Cartesian coordinates are meaningless to a network, every MLP starts from a representation invariant to translation, rotation, and permutation — hand-crafted symmetry functions (Behler–Parrinello) or learned via message passing (SchNet-style GNNs, and equivariant variants like NequIP/MACE):

None of this erases the core advantage: classical MD is still orders of magnitude cheaper than solving electronic structure at every step — the right tool whenever the question is about bulk behavior rather than the fate of individual electrons.

Cheat sheet

The twelve things to remember

  1. A force field = functional form + fitted parameters; it replaces electrons with springs, angles, and pair potentials.
  2. Harmonic bonds cannot break — that single choice is why classical MD can't simulate chemistry.
  3. Dihedrals are the softest bonded term and drive conformational flexibility.
  4. LJ handles short range; Coulomb needs Ewald/PME — never a naive cutoff.
  5. Tail corrections ≈ 10% of total energy at typical cutoffs — never optional.
  6. U→F via −∇ is the single most important transformation in MD.
  7. Verlet, leap-frog, and velocity-Verlet are equivalent, symplectic, time-reversible.
  8. Δt is capped by the fastest vibration — C–H stretches force Δt ≲ 1 fs unless constrained.
  9. Equilibrate with Berendsen/velocity scaling; produce in NVE or with Nosé–Hoover.
  10. Never mix parameters across force fields — the dihedral + 1–4 balance defines rotational barriers.
  11. Binding and catalysis are free energies: FEP, TI, or umbrella-sampled PMFs, not single-trajectory values.
  12. ML potentials move the accuracy/speed frontier but stay prisoners of the data they trained on.
